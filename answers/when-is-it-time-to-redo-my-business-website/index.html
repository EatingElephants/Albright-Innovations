When is it time to redo my business website?

When it no longer does its three jobs: tell people what you do, show them you're real and current, and make it easy to reach you. If it's more than three years old, awkward on a phone, or shows old hours, prices or photos, it's time to take a hard look.

Last updated October 4, 2026

The signs

  • It's hard to use on a phone: tiny text, pinching to zoom, buttons too small to tap
  • The hours, prices, services or photos are out of date, and fixing them means calling someone
  • The phone number or contact form isn't on every page
  • The photos are stock images instead of your work and your people
  • It takes more than a few seconds to load
  • It says "2019" somewhere, or looks like it should
  • You're a little embarrassed to send people to it

One of these is worth fixing. Two or more, and it's time for a redo. Most people check a business's website before they call. Stanford's research on web credibility found that people judge how trustworthy a site is largely by how it looks. They also trust sites that show signs of being kept current. A dated site doesn't just look old. It costs you the call.

What doesn't need a full redo

Sometimes the structure is fine and the content is stale. If the site works on a phone, loads fast and is easy to edit, you may not need a rebuild. A round of new photos, current services and an honest "about" page can get you most of the way for much less. We'll tell you if that's your situation. A rebuild you don't need isn't a good deal for either of us.

What a rebuild involves

  1. A short conversation about who your customers are and what they need to see to pick up the phone.
  2. The direction first. We tell you what we'd change and why before anything is built, so there are no surprises.
  3. The build. Usually a handful of pages: home, services, about, contact, and reviews or gallery. Built to load fast and work on a phone first.
  4. Your photos, your words. We write it in your voice and use your real work.
  5. Keeping it current. Small updates on a schedule, so it doesn't drift back to where it started.
