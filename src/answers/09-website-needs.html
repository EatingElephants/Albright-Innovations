The six things, explained

  1. What you do. In the first sentence, in plain words. "Residential plumbing in your town" beats "Solutions for all your plumbing needs."
  2. Where you do it. The towns and counties you serve, written out. Google says relevance and distance are part of how it decides which local businesses to show (Google Business Profile Help).
  3. Proof you're real. Photos of your work and your people, your name, your reviews. In eyetracking research, people paid attention to photos of real people and products and ignored generic stock photos (Nielsen Norman Group, 2010).
  4. A way to reach you on every page. Phone number at the top, a short form, and both tappable on a phone.
  5. Current hours and prices where they apply. If something changes with the season, the site should change with it.
  6. A site that works on a phone. About half of U.S. web traffic now comes from phones (StatCounter, September 2026). Big text, big buttons, fast loading.

Page by page

HomeWhat you do, where, for whom, one photo of real work, and the phone number. People often leave a web page within 10 to 20 seconds, so say what you do and how to reach you right away (Nielsen Norman Group, 2011).
ServicesOne short section per service, with what's included and roughly what it costs if you can say.
AboutWho you are, how long you've been at it, why you do it. A photo of you. People hire people.
Reviews or galleryReal reviews with first names, or before-and-after photos with a sentence each.
ContactPhone, email, address or service area, hours, and a form that takes a minute to fill in.

What you can skip

  • A blog you won't keep up. People give more credibility to sites that show they've been recently updated (Stanford Web Credibility Project).
  • A rotating slideshow at the top. Nielsen Norman Group found that auto-rotating slideshows annoy users and make their content easy to miss (Nielsen Norman Group, 2013).
  • Pages for every tiny variation of a service. Fewer, clearer pages are easier to keep current.
  • Pop-ups asking for an email before someone has read a word.
