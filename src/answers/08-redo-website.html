The signs

  • It's hard to use on a phone: tiny text, pinching to zoom, buttons too small to tap
  • The hours, prices, services or photos are out of date, and fixing them means calling someone
  • The phone number or contact form isn't on every page
  • The photos are stock images instead of your work and your people
  • It loads slowly. Google recommends site owners achieve good Core Web Vitals, its measures of loading speed and stability (Google Search Central).
  • It says "2019" somewhere, or looks like it should
  • You're a little embarrassed to send people to it

Our rule of thumb: one of these is worth fixing. Two or more is worth a conversation about a redo. Three in four people look at a local business's website at least half the time before choosing one (BrightLocal, 2019). Stanford's web credibility research found that people quickly judge a site by its visual design, and give more credibility to sites that show they've been recently updated (Stanford Web Credibility Project).

What doesn't need a full redo

Sometimes the structure is fine and the content is stale. If the site works on a phone, loads fast and is easy to edit, you may not need a rebuild. A round of new photos, current services and an honest "about" page may be enough, for much less. We'll tell you if that's your situation. A rebuild you don't need isn't a good deal for either of us.

What a rebuild involves

  1. A short conversation about who your customers are and what they need to see to pick up the phone.
  2. The direction first. We tell you what we'd change and why before anything is built, so there are no surprises.
  3. The build. Usually a handful of pages: home, services, about, contact, and reviews or gallery. Built to load fast and work on a phone first.
  4. Your photos, your words. We write it in your voice and use your real work.
  5. Keeping it current. Small updates on a schedule, so it doesn't drift back to where it started.
